What 51 a16z charts say about AI adoption (Aug–Oct 2026)

Spending is compounding. Prices are collapsing. The gap between deployed and delivering is now a permissions problem.

Emil Regard, 30 NorthNov 17, 2026Data: a16z and the sources it cites

Between August 1 and October 30, 2026, a16z published 51 charts on AI adoption across 50 posts. We read every one of them, plus two related pieces from early November, and pulled them into a single picture. The numbers below are a16z’s and the sources it cites; the reading of what they mean for operators is ours.

The short version is a story told from two ends. Usage and spending are compounding while the price of what is being bought keeps falling. The constraint has moved from models to racks, substations and the factories that make electrical gear. And the clearest evidence of real substitution is in the least glamorous places: call centres and security operations. Technology is getting cheaper. Getting value from it isn’t, and the charts show exactly where the gap between deployed and delivering sits.

Six findings

  1. Spending compounds faster than prices fall, and it is concentrated at the top. Firms spend $1,024 per employee on AI, roughly double last year, while blended token prices fell 58% since mid-July. The median company spends $12 per employee a month against $7,500 for the top 1%, a 625x gap.
  2. The bottleneck is physical. Half of every $100 of AI capex goes to chips and a fifth to power. US electrical-equipment capacity is down 9% since 2019 while its prices are up 69%.
  3. Volume moves to open weights; revenue stays at the frontier. About 72% of tokens now run on open-weight models that capture roughly a quarter of spend. Agents, not people, are the majority user: 7.3T against 1.4T tokens a day.
  4. Capability is outrunning its own trend line. The length of software work an agent can finish went from 30 seconds of human time in 2022 to about 17.5 hours by mid-2026. An hour of a computer-use agent costs $6 to $8, against about $10 offshore and $30 to $45 in the US.
  5. Displacement is real but narrow. Coverage of AI job loss is down 65% from its May peak, yet global call-centre employment has flipped from +4% a year to −4%, while other white-collar roles keep growing.
  6. Adoption is broad, agentic depth is thin, and security is the gate. 92% of large enterprises run employee GenAI, but only 34% let agents make changes. 55% of security chiefs name AI-accelerated attacks their biggest worry.

Who is spending what

The Atlanta Fed’s survey of 880 firms puts AI spend at $1,024 per employee, up 96.1%, with 99-to-249-person firms up 141.5% and business and professional services up 98% to about $1,660. The Ramp AI Index shows how uneven that is: $7,500 per employee a month for the top 1% of companies, $660 for the top 10%, and $12 for the median, all three turning upward from January 2026. The top 1% are not a bigger version of the median; they are a different market.

Usage is also spreading far beyond engineering. Since February, Codex users in legal are up 108x, sales and recruiting 41x, marketing 26x and healthcare 24x, against only 5x in engineering (OAI Signals).

Adoption outside the tech office

The charts that travel best outside a venture audience are the least about technology. Books carrying detectable AI text now hold about 40% of observed self-published sales, against near zero in 2023. Clinician and health-system notes documenting AI reached 1,686 per million in 2026, against 13.9 per million where a patient raised it. And ChatGPT had about 50 million paying subscribers in March 2026, level with Uber One. In each case AI is not a new category beside the old one. It is taking share inside it.

Cheaper tokens, dearer racks

a16z calls it the rack spread. Since mid-July, B300 rentals are up 106%, B200 up 65.7% and H100 up 16.9%, while blended token cost fell 58.4% (Ornn). Every GPU tier now rents above its six-year depreciation schedule. Goldman Sachs shows an LLM price index falling to about 10 within 30 months; the PC price index took roughly 15 years to get there.

The pricing question follows directly. Tugce Erten’s Nov 2 a16z essay argues that cheaper intelligence raises the bar for applications: if a direct prompt to a frontier model beats a vendor’s tool, the premium disappears. Her answer is to operate the workflow better than the customer can, make context cheaper to rent than to rebuild, and price at the highest layer of value you can measure, such as a contract reviewed or a ticket resolved. The market already agrees: Morgan Stanley data shows IT services forward P/E falling from about 38 to about 10, and in a16z’s survey of 50 technical buyers, 17 prefer paying per accepted recommendation. The case studies in the essay are vendor-reported, and a16z has invested in some of the companies it cites.

Capability: agents that work for hours

Three separate curves agree. METR’s task-length measure has doubled about every seven months since 2019, reaching 12 hours for Claude Opus 4.6 and roughly 17.5 hours by mid-2026, with wide error bars on the latest points. On OSWorld-Verified, computer-use agents went from 28% to 85% in about a year, past the 72% human baseline. And the cost of an agent hour has fallen below offshore labour before falling below domestic.

Labour: a narrow displacement

The broad AI-jobs panic has faded fast. News reports on AI job loss are down about 65% from a May peak, and AI-citing layoff announcements fell about 90%, from roughly 39,000 in May to about 4,000 in September. But call centres show clean, measurable substitution. Global call-centre employment grew about 4% a year from 2009 and is now running about −4% (Revelio Labs). In 11 of 12 countries it is flat or falling since ChatGPT launched, and the decline has reached lower-middle-income countries, the last holdout. Meanwhile the buildout is creating work: more than 300,000 construction jobs since 2022, with data-centre facilities managers earning 64% more than the same title elsewhere.

The buildout hits physical limits

Texas has 474 GW asking to connect to its grid, 9.5 GW approved and about 4.3 GW running (ERCOT). Of 86 GW of new US capacity planned for 2026, none is nuclear. Grid and electrical-equipment imports are up 69% since 2019 while domestic output fell 7%. The counterweight: a Duke Nicholas Institute estimate that flexible campuses giving up half a percent of annual electricity could add about 98 GW to the largest US grids without a new plant.

Security: adoption outrunning control

This is where the gap between deployed and delivering is most visible. a16z’s survey of 100 CISOs shows how broad deployment already is, and how shallow agent authority remains.

The threat side is moving faster. The share of exploited vulnerabilities attacked on or before disclosure day has gone from 18.7% in 2018 to 86.7% in 2026 to date. Across 21 major software companies, critical and high-severity CVEs never cleared 100 a month for four years, then passed 600 from spring 2026. The AI Security Institute found the best current models can complete all 32 steps of a simulated cyberattack.

Priorities are shifting accordingly: internal AI agent security is up 28 points on 2027 priority lists and the AI SOC up 26. Only 18% run most offensive testing continuously today; 56% expect to within three years.

Update: adoption arrives as chaos

On Nov 3, Replit CEO Amjad Masad summed up the moment in one line: “We’re deep in the chaotic phase of enterprise adoption of agents and vibe coding.” He was sharing a summary of Marc Andreessen’s Oct 26 essay, which argues that the technologies that mattered most, PCs, spreadsheets and email, all entered the office as chaos, brought in bottom-up before IT signed off. The winners were the leaders who reined the chaos in without killing it. Agents are at that stage now: they “run in chat windows, personal accounts, and one-off scripts,” and “nobody decided what they could touch.”

The charts put numbers on it. Breadth is here, with 92% running employee GenAI and 83% running coding agents, but only 34% run agents with write permissions, and 46% of CISOs fear agents acting outside their permissions. That gap is the chaos phase in numbers.

Consumers

Consumers trust AI to help them shop, but not to buy. 44% say AI helps them find better value and 37% bought on an AI recommendation in the past year, yet 42% would not trust AI to purchase on their behalf, and only 25% would let it book travel (Statista). The consumer agent is a research assistant with a checkout problem.

What this means for operators

  • Price the outcome, not the hour or the token. When an agent hour costs less than offshore labour and token prices keep falling, billing for effort is a shrinking business. For a managed service or a connectivity provider, the test is the same as for any AI application: price the completed outcome, such as a device kept patched or an overage prevented.
  • Permissions are the gate to agent depth. The distance between 92% adoption and 34% write access is not a model problem. It is governance: deciding what agents can touch, auditing what they did, and rolling out with discipline. That work is what turns deployed into delivering.
  • Security has to move at machine speed. With nearly nine in ten exploited bugs attacked by disclosure day, patching on a monthly cycle no longer matches the threat. Continuous monitoring and testing are becoming the baseline.
  • Plan around physical constraints. Power, electrical equipment and connectivity, not models, now set the pace of the buildout.
